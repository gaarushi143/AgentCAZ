# Research: Spotify

## Company Context
**Spotify Technology S.A.** is the world's largest audio streaming platform, operating a freemium model across Premium (subscription) and Ad-Supported segments. As of Q3 2025, Spotify reported <cite index="1-1">Monthly active users (MAU) -- 713 million, up by 17 million sequentially, surpassing guidance by 3 million.</cite> Premium subscribers reached <cite index="1-2">281 million, increasing by 5 million for a 12% year-over-year growth</cite> in Q3 2025, following Q2 2025 where <cite index="4-1">Spotify Premium subscribers climbed to 12% year over year to 276 million, a net gain of 8 million in the quarter. Total MAUs grew 11% to 696 million.</cite>

Spotify has aggressively diversified beyond music into podcasts, audiobooks, and now video. Founder/CEO Daniel Ek has positioned podcasts and video as the next growth vector as music streaming growth matures. Spotify's catalog has grown enormously: the platform now hosts <cite index="60-0">nearly 7 million podcast titles, with the video podcast library including nearly 500,000 shows</cite>. Video is the fastest-growing format — <cite index="60-0,61-0">time spent with video content on Spotify in 2025 has more than doubled year-over-year, and more than 390 million users have now streamed a video podcast on the platform, up 54% year-over-year</cite>. Management sees cross-platform exposure (e.g., publishing clips to YouTube) as a discovery flywheel: <cite index="61-1">having Spotify podcasts on YouTube increased awareness about the shows and their origins, which then resulted in net incremental usage on Spotify</cite>.

Monetization ambitions are explicit: Spotify's internal strategy targets <cite index="6-1">increasing podcast revenue to $1 billion by 2026</cite>, and the company has invested heavily in creators — <cite index="7-0">Spotify paid over $100 million to podcast publishers and creators in Q1 of 2025 alone</cite>. This is part of a broader "Partner Program" launched in 2025 to align creator incentives with platform engagement, including <cite index="5-0">audience-driven payouts from Spotify Premium video engagement in select markets and ad monetization on Spotify Free and other listening platforms</cite>.

Despite this investment, podcast engagement lags dramatically behind music: Spotify's podcast MAU is a small fraction of total MAU, and Spotify has recently been **overtaken by YouTube** as the most-used podcast platform among weekly consumers — a strategically alarming trend given how much Spotify has invested in becoming "the" podcast platform since 2019 (Gimlet, Parcast, Anchor, exclusive deals with Joe Rogan, etc.). This case sits squarely at the intersection of a maturing core business (music) and an underperforming growth bet (podcasts) where the specific failure point is *discovery*, not content supply — Spotify has abundant content (7M titles) but a broken bridge between that supply and listener demand.

## Market Analysis
**TAM (Global Podcasting Industry):** Estimates vary by research methodology, but converge on rapid growth. One estimate values the market at <cite index="8-0">$47.75 billion in 2025, growing to $62.06 billion in 2026 at a CAGR of 30%</cite>. Another puts the podcasting market at <cite index="34-0">USD 28.2 Billion in 2025, expected to reach USD 191.3 Billion by 2034 at a CAGR of 23.71%</cite>. A third estimate: <cite index="9-0">the podcasting market size was valued at USD 30.81 billion in 2024, projected to grow from USD 38.96 billion in 2025 to USD 327.83 billion by 2034 (CAGR 26.7%)</cite>. Regionally, <cite index="34-1">North America commands 38.5% of global podcasting revenues in 2025, bolstered by the world's most mature podcast advertising ecosystem</cite>.

**Listener base (SAM):** Global reach is large and growing — <cite index="53-0">over 7 hours per week listening on average</cite> for engaged fans, with the industry overall valued around $40 billion in 2025 (ad spend ~$4.5B). In the U.S. specifically, <cite index="30-0">an estimated 158 million consumers listened to a podcast in the last month as of 2025, up from 135 million in 2024</cite>.

**Spotify's SOM (current captured engagement):** This is the crux of the case. Spotify's *podcast-specific* MAU has grown from 19.9M (2020) to 42.4 million in 2025, against a total platform MAU north of 700 million — meaning only ~6% of Spotify's total user base regularly engages with podcasts, despite podcasts being promoted on the home screen for years. Corroborating the engagement gap, MIDiA consumer research found <cite index="17-0">average total time spent listening to podcasts (1,042 minutes) was just 2.8% of average total time spent listening to music (37,229 minutes)</cite> among surveyed users.

**Market share dynamics — Spotify is losing, not gaining, ground.** A key inflection: <cite index="26-0">Edison found 37% of weekly podcast consumers now select YouTube as the service they use most often for podcasts, up from 31% two years ago. Spotify, which previously led the ranking, has been overtaken as video consumption expands.</cite> Platform share among weekly U.S. podcast consumers as of April 2025: <cite index="25-0">YouTube at roughly 39%, followed by Spotify at 21% and Apple Podcasts at 8%</cite>.

**Where discovery actually happens (and it's not inside podcast apps):** This is the most important market-level insight for the case. Independent research shows <cite index="45-0">YouTube is now podcasting's front door... 40% of listeners discovered a favorite show there, while YouTube and social media drive 61% of all favorite-show discovery.</cite> That means in-app discovery mechanisms across ALL podcast-specific platforms (Spotify included) are fighting for a minority of discovery moments — most discovery has already left the podcast app entirely for social/video platforms optimized for short-form virality.

**Demand signal — listeners are actively hunting, but not finding it in-app:** <cite index="46-0">Half of listeners say they looked for a new podcast during the past month, while 45% started a new show in the past three months. Among limited-series listeners, three in four say they seek a replacement when a series ends.</cite> This proves the problem is not lack of listener intent to discover — it's that Spotify's in-app discovery surface isn't capturing that intent.

**Growth trend / inflection point:** Video podcasting is the clear industry inflection — <cite index="52-0">time spent with video content on Spotify more than doubled year over year, and more than 80% of the US top 50 podcasts now publish a video version</cite>. This is reshaping discovery because video enables short-form clip virality (the same mechanism driving YouTube's dominance) in a way pure-audio feeds cannot.

## Competitor Landscape
**1. YouTube (Google) — the emergent leader, not a traditional "podcast app"**
- Positioning: Video-first, clip/short-form native, creator-economy-aligned, free with ads.
- Share: <cite index="26-0">37% of weekly podcast consumers now select YouTube as the service they use most often for podcasts, up from 31% two years ago</cite>; <cite index="25-0">roughly 39% of weekly podcast consumers overall</cite>.
- Differentiation: Wins because discovery happens natively through its existing recommendation engine (Shorts, algorithmic feed) built for billions of pieces of video content — podcasts are just another content type in a system already optimized for discovery-through-clips. <cite index="27-0">YouTube podcasts have more than 1 billion monthly active viewers</cite>.
- Weakness: No dedicated podcast-first UX (no queue/subscription model as clean as audio apps); ad-supported only for most usage; less optimized for background/eyes-off audio listening (driving, commuting).

**2. Apple Podcasts**
- Positioning: Pre-installed on all iOS devices, free, editorially curated (human editors highlight shows), no video-first push.
- Share: ~8% of weekly consumers (April 2025 data), catalog of <cite index="70-0">over 2.82 million podcast titles vs. Spotify's ~7 million</cite>.
- Differentiation: Editorial curation model — human-selected features — is explicitly cited as a potential fix for algorithmic sameness (see Key Insights). Apple doesn't need podcasts to drive subscription revenue, so has less urgency to over-optimize for engagement metrics.
- Weakness: Weaker personalization/algorithmic engine than Spotify or YouTube; not investing heavily in creator monetization at Spotify's pace.

**3. Amazon Music**
- Positioning: Bundled with Prime, increasingly exclusive-content strategy. <cite index="14-0">Amazon Music has gone exclusive, engaging selectively with top producers</cite> rather than being an open platform. Sweetens the deal via <cite index="15-0">ad-free podcast listening as a Prime member perk with a growing library of ad-free shows from publishers like CNN, NPR, and The New York Times</cite>.
- Differentiation: Distribution leverage via Prime bundle (frictionless cross-sell to ~200M+ Prime members) rather than superior discovery/recommendation tech.
- Weakness: Smaller standalone podcast identity; discovery is bundle-driven, not algorithm-driven.

**4. iHeartRadio / iHeartMedia**
- Positioning: Legacy radio broadcaster leveraging terrestrial radio promotion + owned podcast studio network (largest podcast publisher by some measures).
- Traction: <cite index="text">iHeartMedia's podcast revenue grew 24.5% year over year</cite>, and the company <cite index="text">claims the #1 podcast audience as measured by Podtrac and Triton</cite> (per company statements).
- Differentiation: Cross-promotion via 850+ owned radio stations acts as an offline/on-air discovery channel competitors lack.
- Weakness: Distribution is its own (iHeartRadio app + radio), not solving discovery for content on other platforms like Spotify.

**5. Spotify itself (incumbent within its own product)**
- Positioning: Largest catalog (7M titles) + largest algorithmic personalization stack (built for music) extended to podcasts, plus AI DJ and audiobook cross-recommendations.
- Differentiation attempt: Video podcasts, AI-personalized "DJ" playlists, and audiobook-style embedding-based recommendation models (HGNN architecture blending podcast/audiobook interaction embeddings).
- Core weakness (the crux of the case): Algorithmic recommendation optimized for platform-level retention, not individual discovery of niche/long-tail content. As one analysis frames it: <cite index="19-0,19-1,19-2,19-3">Apple Podcasts and Spotify recommend the same shows to everyone because their recommendation systems are optimised for retention and engagement at the platform level, not for genuinely useful discovery at the individual level... The result is a strange consensus reality where every "podcast person" knows the same ten shows, regardless of what they actually need to learn.</cite> Spotify's own programmatic investment reinforces this: <cite index="19-4">Spotify pays out programme deals worth tens of millions to a small number of shows</cite>, concentrating discovery surface area on already-famous shows (Rogan, Theo Von, Mel Robbins topped 2025 Wrapped charts) rather than the long tail.

**Competitive summary:** Spotify does not compete with Apple/Amazon/iHeart on catalog size or creator payouts — it already leads there. It is losing on *discovery mechanics*: YouTube's clip-native, algorithmically mature short-form discovery layer and Apple's human-editorial layer both outperform Spotify's music-derived collaborative-filtering approach when applied to podcasts, a fundamentally different content type (long-form, low-repeat-listen, high cold-start problem for new shows).

## User Segments
**Segment 1: "Casual Music-First Listener" (the ~94% who don't engage with podcasts)**
- Profile: Long-time Spotify Premium/Free user whose habitual behavior is built entirely around music playlists, Discover Weekly, and Daily Mix. Podcasts appear in their home feed but are rarely clicked.
- Job-to-be-done: "Help me relax/focus/get energized during a specific activity (commute, workout, work) without me having to think about what to pick." Music satisfies this job trivially (30-second skip cost, huge catalog familiarity). Podcasts carry much higher perceived risk — a 45-60 minute commitment to an unfamiliar host/topic.
- Pain point: Zero-friction switching cost exists for music (skip song) but does NOT exist psychologically for podcasts (an unfinished, unfamiliar episode feels like "wasted time"), so users default back to what they know. Spotify's recommendation surfaces top-of-funnel podcast tiles that look identical in format to music tiles, but require an entirely different trust/commitment decision — a UX-to-cognitive-load mismatch.

**Segment 2: "Podcast-Curious Explorer" (the churn-prone / one-and-done tryers)**
- Profile: Users who *have* clicked into a podcast episode (perhaps via a Wrapped prompt, a friend's share, or a homepage nudge) but abandon after 1-2 episodes and never return to podcasts.
- Job-to-be-done: "Find a show that reliably fits **my** specific niche interest (not the mainstream top-50) — e.g., true crime in my local region, a niche B2B topic, a specific hobby — without me having to search hard." This is directly evidenced by: <cite index="19-1,19-2">recommendation systems... diverge sharply once you get past the top 50 shows... shows that are excellent in narrow verticals... effectively do not exist in the discovery layer.</cite>
- Pain point: Cold-start problem — Spotify's collaborative filtering needs prior listening signal to recommend well, but a first-time podcast listener has none, so the algorithm defaults to popularity (same top 10-50 shows everyone already knows), which doesn't match the "explorer's" actual niche need, leading to disappointment and abandonment.

**Segment 3: "Cross-Platform Fan" (already engaged, but engaging elsewhere)**
- Profile: Active podcast listener who discovers new shows on YouTube/TikTok/Instagram (per <cite index="45-0">40% discovering favorite shows on YouTube, 61% via YouTube+social combined</cite>) but listens passively via Spotify for background/audio-only convenience (driving, gym) when a video isn't needed.
- Job-to-be-done: "Let me consume the *audio version* of content I already discovered elsewhere, seamlessly, without re-discovering it." This user's discovery job is already being fulfilled — just not by Spotify.
- Pain point: Spotify's search/onboarding friction when a user arrives from an external link/share (e.g., a TikTok clip references "the podcast where they talked about X") — if the exact episode/show isn't easily findable or if Spotify's own recommendation engine doesn't reinforce the habit loop afterward (no proactive next-episode nudge), the user's engagement stays shallow and transactional rather than becoming a habit.

**Cross-segment insight:** All three segments point to the same root design flaw — Spotify built one algorithmic engine (collaborative filtering + embeddings tuned on music behavior: short items, high repeat-listen, low commitment) and grafted it onto podcasts (long-form, low repeat-listen, high commitment, high cold-start difficulty) without redesigning the actual discovery *mechanism* (only the recommendation *content*) for the format's different consumption psychology.

## Key Insights
**1. The engagement problem isn't a content supply problem — Spotify already has 7M+ podcast titles (more than Apple's 2.82M) — it's a discovery *routing* problem.** The platform has abundant long-tail supply that the recommendation system structurally cannot surface once you get past the top ~50 shows, per direct analysis: <cite index="19-1,19-2">recommendation systems are optimised for retention and engagement at the platform level, not for genuinely useful discovery at the individual level. The two objectives diverge sharply once you get past the top 50 shows.</cite> This reframes the case from "build better recommendations" to "build a fundamentally different discovery mechanism for the long tail" (e.g., editorial curation, topic/intent-based search, community curation) rather than more collaborative filtering.

**2. Spotify is being disintermediated at the TOP of the discovery funnel, not just losing engagement inside its own app.** <cite index="45-0">YouTube and social media drive 61% of all favorite-show discovery</cite> — meaning most of the "discovery" job has already left podcast-dedicated apps for short-form video/social platforms before a listener even opens Spotify. This means Spotify's improvement opportunity may lie less in its internal recommendation algorithm and more in becoming a superior **capture point** for discovery that started elsewhere (deep-linking, clip-to-episode conversion, "shazam for podcast clips") — a fundamentally different PM solution space than "improve the algorithm."

**3. There is a massive, measurable "engagement chasm" between music and podcasts on the SAME platform with the SAME user base — proving the gap is behavioral/product, not audience-availability.** Only ~42.4M of Spotify's 700M+ MAU regularly engage with podcasts (~6%), and even among those who do, <cite index="17-0">average time spent listening to podcasts is just 2.8% of average time spent listening to music</cite>. Since it's the same users on the same app, the gap cannot be explained by "podcasts aren't appealing" (external data shows 158M US monthly podcast listeners exist) — it must be explained by Spotify's specific in-product discovery-to-habit conversion failing, which is a solvable PM problem, not a market-demand problem.

**4. Video is quietly becoming the *discovery* mechanism, not just a content format — and Spotify's own data proves the flywheel works when activated.** Management explicitly linked video/YouTube presence to platform discovery: <cite index="61-1">having Spotify podcasts on YouTube increased awareness about the shows and their origins, which then resulted in net incremental usage on Spotify.</cite> Combined with <cite index="390million-stat">390 million users having streamed a video podcast, up 54% YoY</cite>, this suggests Spotify's own in-app short-form/clip surfacing (not just full video episodes) is an underexploited discovery lever — most of Spotify's video investment has gone to full-episode watch experiences, not TikTok/Shorts-style clip discovery *within* Spotify itself.

**5. Active listener discovery intent is high and currently unmet — this is not a demand-generation problem but a supply-matching problem.** <cite index="46-0">Half of listeners looked for a new podcast in the past month; 45% started a new show in the past three months; three in four limited-series listeners seek a replacement when a series ends.</cite> This is a golden, underexploited moment: Spotify could build proactive "replacement show" triggers the moment a limited series or show ends (a clear, high-intent, low-cold-start moment) rather than relying on generic homepage recommendation rows — a specific, targetable feature opportunity most competitors aren't optimizing for.
